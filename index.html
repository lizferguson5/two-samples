Is the difference real? The two-sample t-test

Lizards in sun vs. shade, frogs in parks vs. wetlands, blackbirds before vs. after a hormone implant. Each time, two groups have different averages. The t-test tells you whether that gap is bigger than you'd expect from ordinary variation alone.

1Which t-test do I need?

A t-test asks: "Are the means of two groups statistically different from each other?" Before you calculate anything, you have to know how your two groups are related.

Independent samples t-test

Compares two separate groups of different individuals. Each individual appears in only one group.

Lizards from grassland vs. lizards from forest. Treated cells vs. untreated cells.

Paired samples t-test

Compares the same individuals measured twice (before/after, or under two conditions). You test the difference within each individual.

The same 13 blackbirds before and after a testosterone implant.

Ask yourself: could I draw a line connecting each measurement in group 1 to one specific measurement in group 2?

The six steps of every t-test

  1. State the hypotheses. H₀: μ₁ = μ₂ (no difference). HA: μ₁ ≠ μ₂ (there is a difference).
  2. Choose a significance level (α), usually 0.05.
  3. Calculate the t-statistic.
  4. Determine the p-value (or compare t to the critical value from the table).
  5. Compare p to α and draw a conclusion. p ≤ α → reject H₀. p > α → fail to reject H₀.
  6. If p is close to α, check the effect size to see how big the difference really is.

2Signal vs. noise: what the t-statistic measures

The t-statistic is a ratio. The top is the signal: how far apart the two means are. The bottom is the noise: how much those means would wobble by chance (the standard error). A big t means the gap is many standard errors wide.

t = x̄₁ − x̄₂√( s₁²/n₁ + s₂²/n₂ ) signal (difference in means)
÷ noise (standard error, SE)

5Equal variance? Student's vs. Welch's t-test

The independent t-test has two assumptions to check first: each group's data should be roughly normal, and the two groups should have similar spread (homogeneity of variance, "similarity in variation in a dataset").

Student's t-test (pooled, equal variance)

Assumes both groups have the same variance. df = n₁ + n₂ − 2. This is the test in the worked example and the frog problem.

Welch's t-test (unequal variance)

Does not assume equal variances. It adjusts the df downward when spreads differ, which makes it more cautious and more reliable.

How do you know? Levene's test

Levene's test checks whether the groups have the same variance in the population. It has its own hypotheses:

H₀: the groups have equal variances.
H₁: the groups have different variances.
Levene p > 0.05: no evidence the variances differ. Use Student's t-test.
Levene p ≤ 0.05: the variances differ. Use Welch's t-test.

In jamovi: Independent Samples T-Test → tick Homogeneity test and Normality test under Assumption Checks. If Levene's test is significant, tick Welch's under Tests. The Data Lab tab runs Levene's test on any data you enter.

6Paired t-test: red-winged blackbirds

High testosterone can help attract mates, but too much may weaken immunity. Researchers measured antibody production (ln[mOD/min]) in 13 male red-winged blackbirds before and after a testosterone implant. Did antibody production change? α = 0.05, two-tailed.

Data and differences

Each line is one bird

There's a lot of variation between birds, but each bird changes only a little. A paired test focuses on the change within each bird and ignores the bird-to-bird differences.

t =d̄ − 0sd / √n d̄ = mean of the differences, sd = SD of the differences,
n = number of pairs, df = n − 1

7Statistically significant vs. biologically important

Statistical significance

The difference is unlikely to be due to random variation. That's what p tells you.

Biological significance

The effect size is meaningful for the system you study. A 0.1 g difference in a 14 g frog may be real but not matter.
